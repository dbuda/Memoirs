TITLE: An Interview with Priya Bennett
SUBJECT: Priya Bennett, Nora Bennett's daughter, also known as Pree to her family
INTERVIEWER: Helen Ward
DATE OF INTERVIEW: 2026-02-21
NOTE: This is fictional training data for the Memoirs demo. All names, places and events are invented.

[00:00:09] HELEN: What is your earliest strong memory?

[00:00:17] PRIYA: The smell of my grandmother's sewing room, although she was in Liverpool and I was too young to know that was unusual. My mother, Nora, tells me I was born in January 1979. She sometimes says 1978 because I was born just after midnight on New Year's Day. The official hospital paper says January 1, 1979, so I use that. My family still celebrates on the evening before because Mum thinks midnight birthdays deserve two dinners.

[00:01:10] PRIYA: My father was Alan Bennett. At home he was Al. He repaired refrigeration systems, and when I was little I thought he made cold air by hand. My brother Daniel was born about two years after me, in 1981. He disagrees with everything I say about our childhood, including the colour of our front door.

[00:02:04] HELEN: When did you leave England?

[00:02:11] PRIYA: I was seven, so it must have been late 1986 or very early 1987. We arrived in Adelaide during the hot season. I remember the airport carpet, my mother's blue suitcase and being told not to touch a lizard on the garden wall. Mum says we arrived in February 1987, but I remember Christmas decorations in the shop windows. My memory is probably mixing two trips.

[00:03:02] PRIYA: The first year was difficult. Dad worked constantly and Mum went from cleaning jobs to the school library. She was always tired, but she still read to us every night. We lived in Woodville. There was a lemon tree in the backyard, and Daniel and I used to throw lemons over the fence until a neighbour returned them in a bucket.

[00:04:06] HELEN: What was The Lantern?

[00:04:12] PRIYA: It was Mum and Mei's cafe. Mei Tan was Mum's closest friend. I called her Auntie Mei, although she was not related to us. Mum says they met at English class in 1988. Auntie Mei says it was 1989, after a community cooking course. I believe Mum because she remembers the dress she wore, but that is not scientific evidence.

[00:05:03] PRIYA: The cafe opened in 1992, I am almost certain. I was thirteen. I helped after school, mostly wiping tables and writing the specials on a blackboard. Mum says it opened in 1991 because she connects it with a winter when the power went out for three days. The opening night was not glamorous. The sign was painted by a friend, the second-hand refrigerator leaked, and Dad had to fix the back door.

[00:06:01] PRIYA: People came because the place felt safe. There was a teacher who always sat near the window, a retired couple who shared one slice of cake, and nurses from the hospital who arrived after midnight. Mei knew everyone's food preferences. Mum knew everyone's divorce stories.

[00:07:00] HELEN: Was there an important event connected with the cafe?

[00:07:06] PRIYA: In 1996, a woman named Evelyn lost a small red suitcase outside the cafe. It contained photographs and letters from her husband. Mum and Mei helped her search for it for two days. A bus driver eventually returned it. The story mattered because the cafe became a kind of noticeboard for the neighbourhood. People trusted Mum with things.

[00:08:04] PRIYA: The Lantern closed in 2003. I remember being twenty-four and coming back from Melbourne to help clear the shelves. Mum says I was twenty-five, which is another one of our family arguments. Auntie Mei's mother was ill, and Dad's health had deteriorated. Closing was the sensible decision, but Mum cried when they took down the sign.

[00:08:53] HELEN: What happened after that?

[00:09:00] PRIYA: Mum returned to the school library. A few years later she started collecting oral histories from older residents. That work changed her. Before, she thought she had spent her life doing small things. Afterward, she understood that remembering is work. She kept notebooks with dates, names and little corrections in the margins.

[00:09:48] PRIYA: I work in community health now. I learned from Mum that people often tell you the important thing sideways. They begin with a bus route or a recipe and eventually reach the thing they were afraid to say. If a memoir is going to be made from conversations, the system must not flatten those details into a list of facts. The details are often where the truth is.
